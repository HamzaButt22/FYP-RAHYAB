# Career games abound, but none validate Pakistani stream choice

No published system does what the planned app does: a mobile game that infers a validated interest and aptitude profile from Pakistani students in classes 9-12 and turns it into a stream recommendation. Across roughly 30 career games and gamified tools published between 2014 and 2026, only about five tested real school samples with digital rigour: like2be in Switzerland (n=809), Kazakhstan's GCG (n=1,865), Ecuador's RIASEC narrative game (n=200), a UK nursing game (n=137) and the NEFELE model. None comes from South Asia except one low-tier Indian paper aimed at graduates. Almost none reports a convergent-validity coefficient for game-derived *interest* scores in adolescents. The measurement literature supports a hybrid design: interest items wrapped in story and swipe mechanics, plus genuine puzzles for aptitude. Gamified questionnaires converge with self-report better than behaviour-inferring games (pooled Fisher's Z .60 vs .42). The headline meta-analytic figure, Fisher's Z .516 (about r = .47), comes entirely from *adult* personality samples with extreme heterogeneity. On recommendations, the evidence favours a transparent knowledge-based or rule-based scorer over the accuracy-chasing classifiers that dominate the stream-selection literature, because those classifiers learn from what students *chose* and so reproduce parental, prestige and gender bias. On effectiveness, the guidance literature is consistent: apps work better with a counsellor or structured session attached, and with at least three "critical ingredients". In Pakistan, parents are the strongest single influence on choice. Most of these findings are good news for the plan and confirm the earlier report's architecture. A few numbers in that report need correcting, and the missing validation evidence is itself the publishable contribution.

## How to read the evidence in this review

The notes behind this review searched Google Scholar-adjacent indexes (OpenAlex), IEEE Xplore, ACM, Springer, Elsevier, MDPI, Frontiers, ResearchGate and Pakistani journals. A large share of entries were read **only at abstract level**, so every claim here is tagged. **[FT]** means full text was read. **[ABS]** means abstract or metadata only. **[SEC]** means the figure comes from a secondary summary or search snippet and needs checking before it goes into a thesis. Venue quality is also marked. **PR** means peer-reviewed in a recognised journal or conference. **PR-low** means peer-reviewed but in a low-tier regional venue. **Low/predatory** means the venue is widely regarded as questionable (for example IJATCSE, IJISRT, CANA). **NR** means not peer-reviewed (Zenodo, OSF, theses, repositories). The Pakistani journal tiers were not checked one by one against HEC's HJRS list. Newer outlets such as *Social Science Review Archives*, *Research Journal for Social Affairs* and *Social Sciences Spectrum* should be weighted lightly until checked. Only a handful of key sources were read in full text: the Fadillah meta-analysis, the Ramos-Villagrasa review, the Kazakhstan GCG paper, Georgiou et al.'s gamified SJT, Quiroga et al. on video games and intelligence, and Shute's Portal 2 and persistence studies.

## About 30 career games exist; five tested real school samples

The published systems fall into two clusters. **Exploration games** broaden occupational horizons without recommending anything to the individual: like2be, MeTycoon, VR job try-outs, the nursing and STEM games. **Assessment games** infer RIASEC interests or competence and recommend: the Ecuador narrative game, Kazakhstan GCG, the Korean life simulation, MyUniPath, the Brazilian RIASEC islands. The Pakistani app belongs in the second cluster, which has the weakest evidence: mostly prototypes, few samples and almost no validity statistics. The sources are linked in the table below.

Six design patterns recur across these papers. They are narrative situational choices mapped to RIASEC, job try-out quests, a themed world per RIASEC type, persona reveals with XP and streaks, counsellor-led card games, and reflection prompts. Engagement is reliably good: SUS around 76, IMI 5.6/7 and satisfaction 4.2-4.5/5 ([Tokzhigitova 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1643246/full)). **Engagement does not equal effect.** like2be's structural model found that cognitive stimulation, not enjoyment or flow, drove learning, and that the game worked best inside a teaching concept ([Keller et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci13010016)). Minecraft modules barely moved STEM interest ([Tablatin 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1127984/pdf)). The large Indonesian card-game literature validates products by expert rating percentages (91-98% "validity"), not by outcomes ([Zahrani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.29210/026767jpgi0005)), and should not count as evidence.

The reviews confirm how thin the field is. **Parola et al. (2023)**, the only core systematic review, found just **7 studies (2008-2021)** and concluded that evidence is limited but points toward gains in career adaptability ([Parola et al., BIT](https://doi.org/10.1080/0144929x.2023.2177822)). No meta-analysis of career games exists. A girls-in-CS gaming review of 25 papers identified personalisation, collaboration and a **female lead character** as the design factors that help ([Sharma et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1875952120300951/pdf)). That is directly relevant to steering Pakistani girls toward ICS and engineering.

### Pakistani and South Asian systems are classifiers for older students

Pakistani computing papers target university students or job-seekers, not Matric or Intermediate group choice. Saeed et al.'s CNN career recommender for engineering students reached 84% accuracy against 81% for content-based filtering ([IEEE ICIC 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/icic53490.2021.9715788)). Fatima et al. inferred MBTI from users' text with up to 93% accuracy, but MBTI has weak psychometric support ([MUET 2022](https://doi.org/10.22581/muet1982.2202.18)). Mahboob et al. found that pre-university education shapes CS graduates' career tracks ([MUET 2024](https://doi.org/10.22581/muet1982.3343)). The only Pakistani high-school system is a **Zenodo record (NR)** of a web counselling system that informs parents of aptitude results. It reports no evaluation, and its abstract closely mirrors an Indian paper ([Nadeem et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18397488)). A 2026 "AI career counselling platform" paper is design-only ([Shakeel et al., IJIST](https://doi.org/10.33411/ijist/1835)). The closest regional analogues are Indian and Bangladeshi stream classifiers, catalogued in the recommender table below.

## Game measurement works, but the headline numbers are adult numbers

### The meta-analytic .516 is a Fisher's Z, from adults, with I² of 94%

**Fadillah, Hidayat & Santoso (2025)** pooled **18 samples from 13 articles** and found a random-effects mean of **Fisher's Z = 0.516 (95% CI 0.391-0.642)**. The authors label it "r", but the forest plot shows it is the Z mean. Back-transformed, it is **r ≈ .47 (CI ≈ .37-.57)**. This back-transformation is the notes' own tanh calculation; the paper does not report it. The inclusion criterion was "adults in pre-employment settings", so **no adolescent samples** were included, and the criteria were personality scales (Big Five, HEXACO, resilience), **not vocational interests**. Heterogeneity was extreme (**I² = 93.9%, τ = .256**), and study-level correlations ranged from **r = .10 to .80** ([Fadillah et al. 2025, IJSG, FT](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/1028/636/6860)). No moderator was significant. Gamified questionnaires pooled at **0.595** against **0.419** for full game-based assessments, a difference that did *not* reach significance (Q = 1.85, p = .174). The data-driven vs theory-driven split was also non-significant. The authors warn against *circular validation*, which means validating a game against the very self-report it aims to replace, and recommend IRT and predictive designs tied to behaviour ([IJSG](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/view/1028)). For context, two different questionnaires measuring the same trait converge at about .31-.56 ([J Bus Psychol 2025, SEC](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-025-10019-6)). An r of .47 is therefore roughly "alternate-questionnaire" quality, not a weak result.

**Ramos-Villagrasa, Fernández-del-Río & Castro's systematic review is from 2022**, published in *Frontiers in Psychology*, not 2023. It covers 34 articles, mostly cross-sectional student samples. Its conclusions: convergent validity is "inconclusive", game-related assessments predicted academic performance in student samples, incremental validity was limited, and they "do not offer sufficient advantages" over conventional tests unless better test-taker reactions are valued for their own sake. Only one study addressed faking. Negative reactions to app-store games came mainly from **bugs and connection errors** rather than content ([Ramos-Villagrasa et al. 2022, FT](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)). A 2025 study found game-based personality assessments are as fakeable as traditional ones ([J Bus Psychol 2025, ABS](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-025-10019-6)).

The most copyable interest/personality template is **Georgiou, Gouras & Nikolaou's (2019) gamified situational-judgement test**. It has 25 scenarios turned into adventure "missions" around a common story, with a progress bar and save/resume. In the gamified version (N=97), convergence with established scales rose to **β = .45-.57**, against **β = .17-.39** for the plain-text SJT (N=321). The factor structure held across N=731 ([Georgiou et al., IJSA, FT](https://skillup.coreconsulting.it/home/pdfs/Georgiou,%20K.,%20Gouras,%20A.,%20&%20Nikolaou,%20I.%20(2019).%20Gamification%20in%20employee%20selection%20The%20developme.pdf)). Skinning good items as story missions did not cost validity, and may have helped.

### Puzzles measure aptitude well; behavioural logs measure persistence weakly

Cognitive aptitude has the strongest game-based evidence, though again in adults. **Quiroga et al. (2019)** had 134 Spanish university students play 10 commercial games on iPad and Wii U. Game-score reliabilities were **.77-.92**, matching or beating the psychometric tests (.74-.84). The latent game factor correlated **.79 with latent g**. However, **4 of the 10 games had floor effects** even in adults, and one was too easy ([Quiroga et al. 2019, FT](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)). In a randomized study of 77 undergraduates, eight hours of Portal 2 beat Lumosity on problem solving and spatial ability (d=.64). Short forms had modest reliability: **12 Raven items gave .65-.70 and 6 mental-rotation items gave .65**, projected to about .90 only at 30-60 items ([Shute, Ventura & Ke 2015, FT](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)). The lesson for a phone app is concrete. Each aptitude needs roughly 20-30 calibrated items, or adaptive selection, before a score is stable enough to drive a recommendation.

The only adolescent stealth-assessment evidence is sobering. In Shute's Physics Playground with US 8th-9th graders, in-game persistence (time on unsolved or hard problems) correlated only **r = .22 with an external persistence task**. A later study of 137 students found persistence time correlated **r = −.80 with attempt counts**, a construct-overlap warning, and depended heavily on problem difficulty ([Rahimi, Shute & Zhang 2021, FT](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/HLM.pdf)). A Philippine replication cautioned that such markers can capture unproductive "wheel spinning" ([ICCE 2020](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/download/1004/939/1836)). Behavioural log signals should therefore stay explanatory, never decisive.

### No adolescent coefficient exists for a game-based RIASEC measure

For *interests*, the evidence is thinnest exactly where the app needs it. A Cal State thesis ran a multitrait-multimethod validation of a game-based interest assessment, but its coefficients were inaccessible ([CSU, ABS](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/concern/theses/v118rg29s)). A 120-person pilot with university students and young adults concluded that its mini-games needed to be more discriminating ([Academia.edu, SEC](https://www.academia.edu/53379662/Are_serious_games_a_reliable_approach_to_the_assessment_of_vocational_interests)). The benchmark for "good" convergence among established inventories is **.24-.71 (SDS vs Strong) and .31-.80 (SDS vs O*NET Interest Profiler)** ([ResearchGate, SEC](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291016202_Self-Directed_Search_Response_Project)). Adolescent anchors that the app could validate against do exist. The **18REST** 18-item RIASEC short form was validated in Brazilian adolescents with gender invariance ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6967077/)). A picture-based inventory (3IP) offers an iconographic precedent for swipe cards ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4873077/)). A Scottish study confirmed the RIASEC circumplex in 1,306 secondary pupils ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8462744/)). Two cautions apply. Interests in early adolescence have both trait and **state** components ([Gfrörer et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08902070211035630)). And the Holland hexagon did not fit in the Urdu Career Key study of 179 students ([Jones, Fouad, Ismail, Munaf & Khan 2014, PJPR](http://pjprnip.edu.pk/index.php/pjpr/article/view/294)), nor in a Slovak adolescent sample ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338004875_Validity_of_Holland's_theory_in_adolescence_evidence_from_a_Slovak_sample)).

The anxiety benefit of game framing, which is the "doesn't feel like a test" goal, is plausible but adolescent evidence is missing. One crossover study in *tertiary* students found game-based exams lowered test anxiety and improved performance ([Mavridis & Tsiatsos 2017, ABS](https://metatoc.com/papers/97555-game-based-assessment-investigating-the-impact-on-test-anxiety-and-exam-performance)). Kazakhstan's teenagers reported low state anxiety (1.9/5) but had no comparison condition ([Tokzhigitova 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1643246/full)). Collmus & Landers and McChesney et al. found that simply *labelling* a test a game improved reactions, while Landers et al. found the gains over conventional tests minimal ([Ramos-Villagrasa 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)). The app's lower-anxiety claim is a hypothesis to test, not a fact to market.

The pattern is clear. **School-stream classifiers predict the stream a student *chose*, from marks and socioeconomic variables**, so they encode today's parental pressure and prestige. Makond's decision tree lists student gender and parental occupation among its six strongest predictors ([TEM Journal](https://doi.org/10.18421/tem113-44)). Their 72-87% accuracies come from single-site samples of 300-800 students with no external validation, and should not be treated as benchmarks. The best-quality evidence points the other way. Song et al.'s study of 81,267 people found that ML beat RIASEC matching on accuracy but **pushed people toward high-base-rate occupations** ([Personnel Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12529)). In Pakistan, that is the mechanism that would steer girls away from ICS and engineering and push everyone toward Pre-Medical. Lahoud et al.'s head-to-head comparison for Lebanese high-school students found a **knowledge-based hybrid** (ontology + case-based reasoning + user-based CF) worked best ([Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-022-11541-3)). A review of 25 career expert systems found **rule-based designs dominate because of simplicity and broad applicability** ([Gunwant 2022](https://doi.org/10.56059/jl4d.v9i3.648)). No peer-reviewed ML paper on Pakistani FSc, ICS, I.Com or FA group selection exists in OpenAlex.

### Fairness and LLM audits argue for explanation-only AI

No stream-selection paper reports a fairness audit. In career and major collaborative filtering, gender bias is documented and partly fixable: neural fair CF produced fairer, more accurate college-major recommendations ([Islam et al., WWW 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3442381.3449904)). Fair algorithms are not enough on their own, though. In a 200+ student study, users **preferred the original gender-biased career recommender** over the debiased one ([arXiv 2021](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2106.07112)). The presentation layer therefore has to counter stereotypes too.

LLMs are not ready to make the recommendation. A pre-registered pilot ran counterfactual audits on GPT-4o-mini. For a balanced profile with no clear academic direction, **93.3% of male-coded responses included a STEM career against 26.7% of female-coded ones**. The gap shrank when the profile had strong academic signals ([Rojas Constaín & Fonseca Correa 2026, OSF, pilot](https://doi.org/10.17605/osf.io/sx7z9)). Six LLMs showed only **35.4% awareness of country-specific context** across ten African countries ([Eze et al. 2026, Computers and Education Open](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeo.2026.100425)). Another study found ChatGPT advised girls more often to validate choices with family or teachers, and offered them more feminised examples ([Castro & Padilla Castillo 2026](https://doi.org/10.7764/cdi.64.98546)). Across six models, LLMs amplified gendered empathy stereotypes beyond human levels when recommending majors ([Ma & Wu 2025, PsyArXiv](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/375tf_v1)). The defensible role for an LLM is to put a deterministic result into Urdu or English prose, restricted to the streams and reasons it is given, with gender-swap tests before launch. The Korean life-simulation system takes exactly this approach ([Seo & Jang 2026](https://doi.org/10.30693/smj.2026.15.9.91)).

Explainability evidence in education is thin but favourable. In a 67-student randomized experiment, course-recommendation explanations that used keywords from students' own past courses increased serendipity and intent to enrol ([Yu et al., UMAP 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3450614.3464483)). A linear or rule-based scorer is explainable by construction ("ICS ranks first mainly because of your logic-puzzle accuracy and your building/tech card choices"). That is a persuasive asset with parents.

## Effective guidance bundles ingredients and keeps humans involved

### Three or more ingredients, plus a counsellor, beat an app alone

The career-intervention meta-analyses converge on a component model. Brown & Ryan Krane's (2000) five "critical ingredients" are written exercises, individualised interpretation and feedback, world-of-work information, modelling, and building support in the student's social network (summarised in Whiston, Rossier & Barón's [2017 chapter](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315714769-3)). Interventions with **three or more ingredients were much more effective** than those with one or two ([CDAA, SEC](https://cdaa.imiscloud.com/Web/Blog/Posts/What-is-best-practice-in-careers-education--An-outline-of-the-evidence.aspx)). Whiston et al. (2017) reportedly added counsellor support, values clarification and psychoeducation, and found expert-facilitated interventions beat self-directed and computer-only ones ([JVB DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2017.03.010); details [SEC]). A 2025 meta-analysis of individual counselling (35 samples) found **g = 0.82** for career outcomes, predicted by psychoeducation about decision-making, cognitive restructuring, written exercises, individualised feedback and attention to obstacles ([J Couns Dev 2025, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1002/joec.12239)). For school-age students specifically, a small meta-analysis found **ES = 0.42 (CI 0.19-0.65)**, with gains concentrated in decidedness ([JEELR 2023, ABS, lower-tier](https://doi.org/10.20448/jeelr.v10i2.4554)).

Computer-assisted guidance works, but less well alone. Secondary accounts of Whiston et al. (2003) report that **counsellor-free computer use did worse than computer use plus counselling** ([Frontiers 2024 review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1461503)). In a 609-student study, only the **CACGS + structured workshop** condition improved career outcomes ([2007 dissertation, ABS](https://doi.org/10.25675/3.020683)). A labour-market-information RCT found larger effects when a counsellor assisted ([CJCD 2021, ABS](https://doi.org/10.82396/cjcd.v17i1.3123)). A 2026 meta-analysis of online career counselling (14 studies, N=1,721) found **g = 1.65**, stronger in **collectivist contexts** and in briefer programmes of six sessions or fewer. Pre-experimental designs were included, so the figure is probably inflated ([J Career Assessment 2026, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1177/10690727261433253)). A randomized study of 957 Chinese 10th graders found a synchronous online programme combining interest assessment and a majors database raised career readiness and reduced decision difficulties ([Frontiers 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.815076)). Most relevant to the game-then-recommend flow is a **2026 lower-secondary RCT**. Self-exploration of interests *followed by* personally ordered career information increased information acquisition, broadened the paths students read about, and shifted attention to non-university routes ([RCT 2026, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1086/742919)).

### Parents are the decisive second user in Pakistan

The Pakistani literature, mostly surveys in regional journals, is consistent on one point. In a *Frontiers in Psychology* study of 200 secondary students, **parental influence (β = .50) outweighed media (β = .26)**, and the model explained 69% of variance in career choice ([Hassan et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.890680)). In Mardan, **46% of 400 secondary students had chosen subjects unrelated to their goals** ([Taj et al. 2025](https://dialoguesreview.com/index.php/2/article/view/311); PR-low, [ABS]). Students in Southern Punjab chose "easy and interesting" subjects shaped by parents' profession and finances ([Asghar & Javed 2018](https://doi.org/10.18178/ijiet.2018.8.11.1145)). In Balochistan, external pressure pushed students toward medicine and engineering, and **counselling access was weakest for girls** ([Sadiq et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.70670/sra.v2i2.279); low-tier). Qualitative interviews describe choices as "enforced or poorly made" ([Keshf & Khanum 2022, PJPR](https://doi.org/10.33824/pjpr.2022.37.1.03)). A KP team explicitly calls for an "assessment mechanism for identification of students' interests and aptitudes" at secondary level ([Zeb et al. 2025, JDSS](https://doi.org/10.47205/jdss.2025(6-ii)44)). That is a ready-made problem statement for a paper. Bangladeshi data mirror these patterns ([Akter et al.](https://doi.org/10.53808/kus.2018.15.1and2.1706-s)).

The strongest Pakistani evidence for *assessment-based* guidance appears in a reputable venue. **Saya, Aslam & Habib (2025)** validated the Career Decision Self-Efficacy scale with **1,445 Pakistani students** and found that students counselled with psychometric testing reported higher decision confidence than those given general guidance ([Psychology in the Schools](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.23552)). The design is comparative, not randomized. No controlled school-level career intervention from Pakistan was found. A small university-level quasi-experiment (n=45) appears in ERIC, attributed only by snippet ([ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1342685.pdf)).

International work shows parents can be targeted directly. A **parent-based utility-value intervention** (brochures and a website) raised high-school STEM course-taking and was linked to STEM career pursuit **five years later** ([PNAS 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1607386114)). Parental *support* most strongly predicts adolescents' career exploration, while interference and disengagement predict indecision. The Parental Career-related Behaviors questionnaire measures all three ([TPM 2016](https://doi.org/10.4473/tpm23.3.6)). Teachers matter too. Carlana found teachers' implicit gender stereotypes led girls to **follow track recommendations into less demanding schools** ([QJE 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz008)), so adult gatekeepers can amplify bias as well as counter it.

On presenting results, there is no adolescent experiment comparing a single recommendation with a top-N list. Indirect evidence favours a short, varied shortlist. Users of an internet decision system benefited more when **satisfied with the length and variety** of their "promising alternatives" ([Gati et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0001-8791(02)00049-0)). A reflection-only app left teens asking for explicit recommendations ([FindMyself 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3626705.3627774)). Its authors warned that guidance systems "bear the risk of imposed decisions".

## The research gap your app can fill

The gap is specific, publishable and genuinely open. First, **no peer-reviewed game-based stream recommender exists for Pakistan or South Asia**. The only local high-school system is an unevaluated Zenodo record ([Nadeem et al.](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18397488)), and the regional stream classifiers are non-game and mostly low-tier. Second, **no published convergent-validity coefficient exists for a game-based RIASEC measure in 14-18-year-olds anywhere**. The meta-analytic base is adult, and the closest adolescent career games report satisfaction rather than validity ([Fadillah](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/view/1028); [Tokzhigitova](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1643246/full)). Third, **no Urdu game-based interest or aptitude measure has been validated with Matric students**, and the one Urdu RIASEC instrument showed structural misfit ([Jones et al. 2014](http://pjprnip.edu.pk/index.php/pjpr/article/view/294)). Fourth, **no randomized or controlled school-level career intervention from Pakistan** and no South Asian computer-assisted guidance outcome study were found. Fifth, **no fairness audit of any stream recommender** and **no evaluation of LLM explanations for stream advice in Urdu** exist. Sixth, **no experiment compares single versus top-N recommendations, or with versus without a parent report**, for adolescents. Finally, the career-game literature almost never measures what happens *after*: actual stream chosen, persistence, satisfaction. DiplomaTec's switching-rate metric is the lone exception ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3625704.3625709)).

A three-study programme would fill these gaps in order. **Study 1 (validation)** gives about 300-600 Matric and Intermediate students, balanced by gender, Urdu/English medium and school type, the game battery plus an anchor inventory: the O*NET Mini-IP under its licence, the public-domain 18REST, or the Career Key Urdu. It would report per-scale convergent and discriminant correlations in a multitrait-multimethod matrix, two-week test-retest reliability, IRT calibration of the puzzle items, circumplex fit, and measurement invariance by gender and medium. The sample range and analyses are this review's inference from the benchmarks above, not a published standard. **Study 2 (effectiveness)** would be a cluster-randomized or quasi-experimental trial with three arms: app alone, app plus one structured counsellor or teacher session with the parent report, and business as usual. The primary outcome would be career decision self-efficacy using the scale already validated in Pakistan ([Saya et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.23552)). Secondary outcomes would be decidedness, parental career-related behaviours ([PCB](https://doi.org/10.4473/tpm23.3.6)), SUS, IMI and state anxiety. Planning around the school-age ES of about 0.42 implies roughly 100 students per arm before the design effect for clustering by school, which is again this review's inference. **Study 3 (follow-up)** would record the actual group chosen, Part-I results and satisfaction 6-12 months later, and audit recommendation rates by gender at matched scores. Suitable venues, judging from where the closest analogues appeared, include *Frontiers in Education*, *International Journal of Serious Games*, *IEEE Transactions on Learning Technologies*, *Education and Information Technologies* and, for the Urdu validation, *Pakistan Journal of Psychological Research*.

Several design decisions follow. The game layer should carry the experience, but scores driving the recommendation should come from items and puzzles with known psychometrics. Free-choice behavioural signals stay at low weight until Study 1 shows they converge. Short quests matter, because completion fell by about 16 points across three levels in Kazakhstan ([Tokzhigitova](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1643246/full)). Robustness matters too: bugs and connection errors, not content, drove negative reactions to app-store assessment games ([Ramos-Villagrasa 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)), so offline-first logging and save/resume are scientific requirements as well as UX ones. Gender should be excluded from fit scoring and used only for auditing. Engagement metrics should never be reported as evidence that the recommendation is valid, given like2be's finding that fun did not predict learning ([Keller 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci13010016)). The app should also ship a counsellor/teacher mode and a printable or WhatsApp summary, because the evidence that blended delivery outperforms the app alone is stronger than any single game-design finding.

## Conclusion

The literature changes the question from "can games recommend streams?" to "can anyone show that a game's recommendation is valid for Pakistani teenagers?" The architecture is already well supported: gamified items, real puzzles, a transparent knowledge-based scorer, an LLM confined to explanation, and a parent-and-counsellor wrapper. What no one has published is adolescent, Urdu, game-based validity evidence, or a controlled test of whether such an app improves decisions in a collectivist, parent-led setting. That absence is both the app's biggest scientific risk and its clearest route to publication. A modest validation study would make it the first of its kind.

Two findings should temper expectations. The best-known validity figures are adult figures with enormous heterogeneity, so a Pakistani app could land anywhere from r ≈ .1 to .8 until it measures itself. And the guidance literature says the app is unlikely to be the active ingredient on its own. Effects come from bundling interpretation, information, modelling and social support, ideally through a counsellor or teacher. The product that wins on evidence is one where the game feeds a conversation, not one where the game issues a verdict.
