# Pakistan and South Asia Studies on Career Guidance, Stream Choice and Career Assessment Tools (Classes 9-12)

Method note: Sources were found through the OpenAlex API (filtered to Pakistani or Indian/Bangladeshi author affiliations, 2010-2026) plus web search. **Almost all findings below come from abstracts retrieved via OpenAlex, not full texts.** Treat effect sizes and percentages as abstract-only unless marked otherwise. I did NOT verify HEC HJRS categories journal by journal; any quality remarks are approximate (based on general knowledge of indexing) and must be checked against hjrs.hec.gov.pk before being stated in the final report. Many of the 2024-2026 Pakistani items appear in newer, low-citation journals (e.g., Research Journal for Social Affairs, Social Sciences Spectrum, Social Science Review Archives, OAELR). These are probably HEC "Y"-category or unrecognized, so weight them lightly. The earlier report already covers Career Key Urdu (2014), Faize & Husain (2024), the 2025 Nishtar JPMA study and the Karachi counselling-awareness study. They appear here only where new detail is added.

## Q1. Factors in stream/group choice, mismatch/regret, and the effect of counselling interventions (Pakistan)

### Takeaway
Pakistani surveys at secondary level keep finding the same drivers of subject/group choice: parents (strongest), teachers, siblings, expected job/income outcomes, perceived ease, and gendered expectations. Formal guidance is mostly absent, and students' career awareness is low. Reported mismatch is high: 46% of secondary students in one Mardan sample had chosen subjects unrelated to their goals. No rigorous controlled trial of a school-level career intervention in Pakistan was found. The evidence for counselling effectiveness is cross-sectional or comparative only.

### Cited Findings
**Factors in choice**
- **Hassan, Luo, Gu, Mushtaque & Rizwan (2022), "Investigating the Parental and Media Influence on Gender Stereotypes and Young Student's Career Choices in Pakistan," *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:890680** (SSCI/Scopus-indexed international journal; 20 citations). The sample was 200 secondary students from government and private schools, chosen by simple random sampling, and analysed by regression. Parental influence predicted gender-stereotype beliefs (β = 0.50) more strongly than media richness (β = 0.26). Parental, media and stereotype variables together explained R² = 0.694 of career choice. There were no significant gender differences in parental or media influence. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.890680)
- **Asghar & Javed (2018), "Investigating Factors Affecting Students' Subject Selection at Secondary School Level," *International Journal of Information and Education Technology* 8(11):815-820.** Southern Punjab; 200 students, 50 teachers and 25 parents; 36-item questionnaire plus parent interviews (mixed methods). Subject choice was driven by students' "potentiality," prior knowledge, parents' profession and family financial position, and students preferred "easy and interesting" subjects. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.18178/ijiet.2018.8.11.1145)
- **Zeb, Ali & Hussain (2025), "Factors Associated with Subjects' Selection Among Secondary School Students in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa," *Global Social Sciences Review* X(III):21-28.** Population 30,439; random sample of 380; chi-square analysis. The factors associated with subject selection were parents, guardians, teachers, siblings, students' personal characteristics and career outcomes. The authors recommend career guidance and orientation seminars at the start of each session. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.31703/gssr.2025(x-iii).03)
- The same KP team (Zeb, Ali & Hussain), using the same 380-student sample in a series of 2024-2025 papers:
  - Personality factors affected career aspirations: interests, "wills," motivations and academic aptitudes. The authors explicitly recommend "assessment mechanism for identification of students' interests and aptitudes" at secondary level. — [JDSS 2025](https://doi.org/10.47205/jdss.2025(6-ii)44)
  - Career outcomes (income, work environment, recognition, future opportunities) significantly affected aspirations. — [Pakistan Social Sciences Review 2025](https://doi.org/10.35484/pssr.2025(9-iii)23)
  - Recommended interventions include trained career guides, parent-teacher collaboration, orientation sessions, and "the use of technology" and social media for guidance. — [Social Sciences Spectrum 2024](https://doi.org/10.71085/sss.03.04.312)
- **Alamzeb, Ali & Ullah (2021), "The Role of Teachers in Career Guidance of Students at Secondary Level in Pakistan," *Journal of Social Sciences Review* 1(2):26-37.** Swat district; 346 of 3,247 high-school students. Teachers act as role models, identify aspirations and abilities, and help with subject selection. The authors recommend building teacher capacity and adding a career-guidance subject. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.54183/jssr.v1i2.9)
- **Khan & Jamil (2024), "The Influence of Extrinsic Factors on Subject Selection among Intermediate Students," *Open Access Education and Leadership Review* 2(1):8-14.** First-year Intermediate science and arts students in public and private institutions, District Mianwali (Punjab); t-tests and Spearman correlation. The main drivers were job prospects, the "contemporary" job market, progression to higher education and media. Gender bias, merit limitations, lack of guidance and school constraints also played a role. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.59644/oaelr.2(1).2024.117)
- **Sadiq et al. (2024), "The Influence of Parents and Society Expectations on Student Career Choices: The Role of Academic Counselling in Balochistan," *Social Science Review Archives* 2(2):2151-2161.** Survey of 281 students. External pressure pushes students toward medicine and engineering on grounds of "job security," and access to counselling is a serious gap, "mainly for female students." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.70670/sra.v2i2.279)
- **Nisar & Rauf (2026), "Career Awareness and Preparedness among Secondary School Students in Quetta," *Bulletin of Multidisciplinary Studies* 3(1):88-94.** Government secondary schools in Quetta; descriptive survey. Career awareness was moderate to low, students had little information on career paths or the labour market, the main information sources were family and teachers, and formal guidance was "mostly not available." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.48112/bms.v3i1.1232)
- **Khalid & Parveen (2024), "Gender Differences in Career Choice Influences … among Secondary School Students in Rawalpindi," *Research Journal for Social Affairs* 2(4):23-30.** The study reports gender differences in influences, but the abstract gives no numbers (abstract-only; low detail). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.71317/rjsa.002.04.0018)

**Mismatch / regret**
- **Taj, Sana, Quddoos & Hussain (2025), "Students' Dilemma in Subject Selection at Secondary Level," *Dialogue Social Science Review*.** Secondary schools in District Mardan (KP); random sample of 400, with science and arts students analysed separately. **46% of students had selected subjects "which did not have relevance with their aspired and desired goals"**, and parental pressure was identified as a problem. — [Article](https://dialoguesreview.com/index.php/2/article/view/311)
- **Keshf & Khanum (2022), "Career Guidance and Counseling Services in Pakistan from the Perspective of Students and Career Services Providers," *Pakistan Journal of Psychological Research* 37(1):39-66** (NIP, Quaid-i-Azam University; long-standing HEC-recognized psychology journal). Qualitative: 25 semi-structured interviews (18 university students aged 17-23 and 7 career-service providers); thematic analysis. Students' academic choices were "misaligned with their personal interests," decisions were "enforced or poorly made," and students procrastinated. Cultural and circumstantial influences dominated, and awareness was limited. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.33824/pjpr.2022.37.1.03)
- **Ahmed, Ahmed & Salahuddin (2019), "How RIASEC Personality Traits Crystallize Occupational Preferences among Adolescents: Match or Mismatch."** Listed by OpenAlex under *Pakistan Journal of Commerce and Social Sciences*; the landing page is at jes.ac.pk, so the venue needs checking. 1,147 students aged 13-21 took Holland's RIASEC scale. RIASEC traits significantly affected occupational preferences, and **gender was a strong determinant** of variation in vocational choice. — [Article](https://jes.ac.pk/index.php/jes/article/view/203)

**Counselling effects**
- **Sahito, Sahito, Alishba & Phulpoto (2025), *Voyage Journal of Educational Studies* 5(1):15-31.** Cross-sectional: 500 students, 50 counsellors and 100 teachers. Students who had received career services reported more decision confidence, better academic performance and more awareness of prospects than those who had not. Barriers were limited resources, few trained counsellors and parental influence. This is correlational only, with selection-bias risk. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.58622/vjes.v5i1.214)
- **Saya & Aslam (2025), "Exploring Career Guidance in Pakistan's Educational Landscape: A Comparative Analysis of Different Counselling Approaches," *Hamdard Educus* 4(1).** Of the students who had acquired career guidance, 643 got it through psychometric testing and 801 through general guidance. Both groups showed improved career decision-making, but **the psychometric-testing group reported more career-decision self-efficacy/confidence**. This is search-snippet only; the full text was unreachable (site DNS failure), and the design appears to be a survey, not a trial. — [Hamdard Educus](https://hamdardeducus.com/index.php/he/article/view/83)
- **Yaqoob, Arif, Samad & Iqbal (2017), "Career counselling and its knowledge among high school students in Pakistan," *Int J Community Med Public Health* 4(7):2261.** Two private Karachi institutes; 500 A-level/Intermediate students, 54% female. 32.6% aimed to become engineers and 29.8% doctors, and 54.8% knew about career counselling. Knowledge was significantly associated with having a counsellor available and attending seminars. This may overlap with the "Karachi counselling-awareness study" in the earlier report; check before citing twice. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.18203/2394-6040.ijcmph20172817)
- Web search found a Pakistani quasi-experiment with university students: 45 students randomized to online counselling, face-to-face counselling or control, with pretest, posttest and two follow-ups. Both interventions increased career development compared with control. — [ERIC, Int J Progressive Education 18(2), 2022](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1342685.pdf). The Pakistani attribution and details come from the search snippet only; verify.

### Inferences
- In Pakistani evidence, parents are the dominant influence. A stream-recommendation app aimed only at students may be overridden at home, so a parent-facing report or "share with parents" mode is strongly justified (cf. Hassan et al. β = 0.50; Zeb et al.; Sadiq et al.).
- Students choose on "easy/interesting" subjects, job security and income. Showing realistic career-outcome information (income, job availability) alongside interest results matches what drives choices.
- The roughly 46% mismatch (Mardan) and the "misaligned/enforced" choices (Keshf & Khanum) give a concrete problem statement for the app. They come from small, local and mostly low-tier surveys, so they should be presented as indicative.
- The only comparative evidence (Saya & Aslam) weakly favours psychometric-assessment-based guidance over general counselling. This supports an assessment-driven app but is not causal evidence.
- Female students have less access to counselling (Balochistan) and show gendered RIASEC patterns. The app should avoid reinforcing stereotypes, for example by not filtering streams by gender.

### Gaps
- No randomized or controlled school-level (grades 9-12) career intervention study from Pakistan was found.
- There is little quantitative urban-vs-rural or socioeconomic-status comparison at secondary level. Asghar & Javed mention family finances, but no effect sizes were found.
- There is no longitudinal regret/switching data (e.g., how many students change group after Matric or drop out of FSc Pre-Medical).
- The abstracts of Khalid & Parveen (Rawalpindi) and the 2024 Zeb et al. intervention paper lack numeric results.

## Q2. Pakistani development, adaptation or validation of career interest, aptitude or personality instruments

### Takeaway
Validated Urdu career instruments are few. They are the Career Key Urdu (2014, already cited) and an Urdu Planned Happenstance Career Inventory (2023, adult-focused). Large Pakistani RIASEC administrations exist (O*NET Interest Profiler, n = 1,503; Holland RIASEC, n = 1,147) but they are usage studies rather than formal validations. A key caution recurs: Holland's hexagon did not fit well in Pakistan. No published psychometric validation of NTS/ETEA aptitude tests for stream guidance was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Jones, Fouad, Ismail, Munaf & Khan (2014), "Adaptation of the Career Key into Urdu," *Pakistan Journal of Psychological Research* 29(2):187-201** (already in the earlier report). Detail to add: 179 students; English and Urdu versions given one week apart; Cronbach α computed by gender and education level. Construct validity was tested with Hubert & Arabie's Randomization Test of Hypothesized Order. "Sound psychometric properties," but "Holland's hexagonal interest structure did not fit expectations," and the indices were low compared with the US sample. — [PJPR](http://pjprnip.edu.pk/index.php/pjpr/article/view/294). A search snippet reports α = .69-.92, Urdu-English correlations of .85-.90 and ages 14-24 — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/313407425_Adaptation_of_the_Career_Key_into_Urdu) (snippet-level).
- **Younus & Ayub (2023), "Translation and Adaptation of Planned Happenstance Career Inventory in Urdu Language," *Journal of Education and Educational Development* 10(1):50-71** (IBA Karachi journal). Two phases: forward/back translation, then psychometrics. Test-retest r = .732 (n = 87). Discriminant validity: r = .018 with a Counterproductive Work Behaviour checklist. Convergent validity: r = .358 with the Urdu Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale. Recommended for individual career counselling. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22555/joeed.v10i1.718)
- **Husain & Faize (2024), "Education without aptitude: mismatched vocational personalities for professional placements," *e-mentor* 106(4):13-21** (Poland; likely the Faize & Husain 2024 item in the earlier report). O*NET Interest Profiler with 1,503 graduating university students. Alignment held in pure sciences, social sciences, architecture, fine arts and psychology, and was absent in CS, management sciences, EE, accounting/finance and mathematics. Males were higher on R/E/C and females on I/A/S. The authors call for aptitude testing and counselling at school and college level. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.15219/em106.1675)
- **Ahmed, Ahmed & Salahuddin (2019):** Holland RIASEC scale with 1,147 adolescents aged 13-21 (see Q1). Gender strongly shaped vocational patterns. — [Article](https://jes.ac.pk/index.php/jes/article/view/203)
- **Noor, Kazemian, Mustafa & Muhabat (2015), "Pilot Study for Validity and Reliability of an Aptitude Test," *Journal of Educational and Social Research*.** An English-language aptitude test (grammar, vocabulary, reading) piloted with only 25 Intermediate students at the University of Gujrat. Reported section "validity" was 84.33%, 91.64% and 52.00%. Methodologically weak; cite only to show how thin aptitude-test validation is. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5901/jesr.2015.v5n3p285)
- Other Urdu adaptations exist for adolescent personality and social-emotional measures, which could serve as companion constructs:
  - Social-Emotional Competence Questionnaire, cross-language validation with 910 public-school adolescents — [PMC10659229](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10659229/)
  - "Translation and Validation of Adolescent Personality Questionnaire: A CFA," *Pakistan Journal of Humanities and Social Sciences* (2025) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.52131/pjhss.2025.v13i2.2779) (metadata only)
  - "Translation and validation of personality inventory for Pakistani youth" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350975772_TRANSLATION_AND_VALIDATION_OF_PERSONALITY_INVENTORY_FOR_PAKISTANI_YOUTH) (title only)

### Inferences
- An app using RIASEC scoring should not assume the US hexagon's adjacency structure (e.g., for "consistency" scores or neighbour-type recommendations) in Pakistan. Scoring on the six scales directly and validating locally is safer (Career Key Urdu finding).
- The gendered RIASEC profiles (Husain & Faize; Ahmed et al.) mean that any norm-referenced scoring should consider gender-specific norms or, preferably, report raw interest profiles without stereotyping.
- There is a clear research gap that the app could fill: no Urdu, game-based interest or aptitude measure validated with Matric students was found.

### Gaps
- No published Urdu Self-Directed Search or Urdu O*NET Interest Profiler validation was found for school students.
- No psychometric studies of NTS, ETEA or MDCAT/ECAT as stream-guidance tools were found. The only such items concern medical admissions (e.g., a 2013 comparison of pre-medical achievement, entrance test and aptitude test scores, listed in OpenAlex/PubMed without an accessible abstract).
- The HEC PRR thesis repository (prr.hec.gov.pk) was not searched directly within the tool budget. MPhil/PhD theses at NIP, QAU or Punjab University on career instruments probably exist but are unverified.

## Q3. Pakistani/South Asian software, expert systems, ML models or apps for career/stream guidance

### Takeaway
Pakistani computing papers on career recommendation mostly target university or engineering students and jobs, not Matric stream choice. They report accuracy (84-93%) on small or self-collected datasets, with no user-outcome evaluation. One Pakistani web system targets high-school stream choice but has only a Zenodo record. The Indian work by Sethi et al. is the closest direct analogue (an ML stream-selection model with 86.72% accuracy).

### Cited Findings
- **Nadeem, Zaidi, Jawed, Haider & Bhutto (2023), "Implementing a Web-based Career Counseling and Guidance System for High School Students," Zenodo record** (not peer-reviewed as deposited; the authors' names suggest Karachi/Sindh). A web app that gathers interests, aptitudes and perspectives through questions, predicts career options across Engineering, Technology, Art and Business, and "inform[s] the student's parents" of aptitude results. The abstract gives no evaluation results. Note: its abstract text closely mirrors Sethi et al. (2018) below. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18397488)
- **Saeed, Sufian, Ali & Rehman (2021), "Convolutional Neural Network Based Career Recommender System for Pakistani Engineering Students," IEEE International Conference on Innovative Computing (ICIC).** A deep NLP CNN (512 hidden layers) on a dataset collected from students across 4-6 engineering disciplines at various universities. Accuracy was 84% versus 81% for content-based filtering. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/icic53490.2021.9715788)
- **Fatima, Gul, Ahmed, Khand & Mujtaba (2022), "A rule-based machine learning model for career selection through MBTI personality," *Mehran University Research Journal of Engineering & Technology* 41(2):185-196** (MUET Jamshoro; Scopus/ESCI-indexed). The model infers MBTI type from user-written text and maps it to career lists, reaching up to 93% accuracy. Caution: MBTI has weak psychometric support. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22581/muet1982.2202.18)
- **Mahboob, Asif & Haider (2024), "Career planning matters: Intelligence-based career path predictions using data mining models - A longitudinal study," *MUET Research Journal* 43(4).** Predicts CS/IT/SE graduates' career tracks from pre-university education, core courses, CGPA, FYP and socioeconomic data, with SMOTE balancing. Finding: **pre-university education directly impacts career choice.** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22581/muet1982.3343). A related 2023 paper by the same group forecasts placement in programming — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22581/muet1982.2302.18).
- **Kiran, Asim & Hassan (2018), "Career and Skills Recommendations using Data Mining Technique … in Pakistani Context," *VFAST Transactions on Software Engineering*.** Association-rule mining that matches job-seekers to the professions of people with similar profiles. Targets job-seekers, not school students. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21015/vtse.v13i3.510)
- **Shakeel, Munawar & Naveed (2026), "Next Generation Career Counseling Platform Powered by Artificial Intelligence," *International Journal of Innovations in Science and Technology*.** A design paper: ML recommendations, NLP chatbot, skill-gap analysis; Angular/Node front end, Django back end, PostgreSQL. No evaluation is reported in the abstract. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.33411/ijist/1835)
- **India: Sethi, Jaiswal & Ansari (2018/2020), "Machine Learning Based Support System for Students to Select Stream (Subject)," *Recent Advances in Computer Science and Communications* 13(3):336-344** (Bentham; 37 citations). Trained on data from students of two streams across institutions (interest, family background, prior education), it reached a best accuracy of 86.72%. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/2213275912666181128120527). Follow-up: Sethi, Rohit & Anand (2025), "MHC-CNN: A CNN Framework for Stream Selection in Secondary Education Using Modified Huffman Coding," Springer CCIS, pp. 253-271 (abstract unavailable) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-08260-2_18).
- **India: Pyari, Mishra & Dua (2016), "A Study of Impact of Aptitude in Mathematics as Stream Selection at Higher Secondary Level," *Issues and Ideas in Education* 4(2):141-149.** 100 students at a government senior secondary school in Agra took the Differential Aptitude Test (DAT). Aptitude significantly affected stream selection; numerical reasoning, clerical speed and accuracy, and mechanical and verbal reasoning predicted the mathematics stream. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.15415/iie.2016.32011)

### Inferences
- The field's evaluation standard is classification accuracy against past choices or outcomes, which can bake in parental and gender bias (the very problem identified in Q1). A game-based app could differentiate itself by evaluating with user-centred outcomes, such as decision self-efficacy pre/post, usability (SUS), and agreement with counsellor judgement.
- Sethi et al. and the Pakistani Zenodo system both include family background and prior education as features, which risks recommending what parents would choose. Keep interest and aptitude primary and report family factors separately.
- The DAT-style aptitude dimensions (numerical, verbal, mechanical, spatial, clerical) are a reasonable blueprint for game mini-tasks. Pyari et al. tie them to choosing the maths stream.

### Gaps
- No Pakistani peer-reviewed paper was found that builds and field-evaluates a stream-guidance tool for Matric/Intermediate students. The INMIC, ICET and ICCIS proceedings were not exhaustively searched; OpenAlex returned ICIC 2021 only.
- No Pakistani FYP-derived publication evaluates a career game with school students.

## Q4. Pakistani studies on gamification/game-based learning acceptance and teens' smartphone access

### Takeaway
Pakistani gamification studies (mostly 2024-2026, low-tier journals) report positive motivation and engagement effects among school students. One older, high-quality study (Khan, Ahmad & Malik 2017, *Education and Information Technologies*) examined digital game-based learning in Pakistani secondary science, including gender differences, but its abstract could not be retrieved. Smartphone access is the binding constraint for girls. As of 2019-20, 65% of males and 25% of females aged 10+ owned a mobile phone. The GSMA 2026 report shows the gap narrowing but still the widest among surveyed countries.

### Cited Findings
- **Khan, Ahmad & Malik (2017), "Use of digital game based learning and gamification in secondary school science: The effect on student engagement, learning and gender difference," *Education and Information Technologies* 22(6):2767-2804** (Springer, SSCI; 216 citations; Pakistani authors). Metadata only; the abstract was not retrievable (Springer paywall; Semantic Scholar rate-limited). The full text should be checked for sample and results before citing specifics. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-017-9622-1)
- Recent Pakistani gamification studies (abstracts not reviewed; titles from OpenAlex):
  - "Impact of Gamification in E-Testing on Students' Engagement and their Learning Outcomes at the Secondary School Level," *Qlantic Journal of Social Sciences* (2026) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.55737/qjss.vii-iii.26542). Directly relevant: gamified assessment at secondary level.
  - "Investigating the Perceptions of Primary School Students on Gamification-based Learning in Mathematics," *Pakistan Journal of Humanities and Social Sciences* (2024) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.52131/pjhss.2024.v12i3.2454)
  - "Secondary Public-School Teachers' Perceptions of Gamification in ESL Instruction: A Qualitative Study," *Forman Journal of Social Sciences* (2024) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.32368/fjss.20240426)
  - "Effect of Gamification on Students Learning Ability: An Experimental Study," *Spry Contemporary Educational Practices* (2024) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.62681/sprypublishers.scep/3/1/26)
  - "The Impact of Gamification on Primary School Students' Motivation and Concept Building," *Journal of Social Research Development* (2025) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.53664/jsrd/06-01-2025-07-80-92)
- **Smartphone and mobile access**
  - **PSLM 2019-20 (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics):** individual mobile ownership among people aged 10+ was 45%, with males at about 65% and females at about 25%. In PSLM 2018-19, urban ownership was 55% and rural 39%. These figures are reported via BR Research's summary of the PBS release — [Business Recorder](https://www.brecorder.com/news/amp/40100651). The PBS district Table 3.2 has the urban/rural-by-sex breakdown — [PBS table](https://www.pbs.gov.pk/sites/default/files//pslm/publications/pslm_district_2019-20/tables/3.2.pdf) (text garbled in extraction; verify).
  - **GSMA Mobile Gender Gap Report 2026 (2025 data), as reported by the press:**
    - 68% of women own a mobile phone versus 93% of men, still the widest ownership gap among surveyed countries.
    - Women's smartphone ownership rose from 30% to 40%, and the smartphone gender gap narrowed from 48% to 30%.
    - Women's mobile internet use rose from 45% to 53%.

    Source: [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.pk/node/2649428/pakistan). Dawn and the Digital Rights Foundation instead describe the ownership gap as falling from 37% to 27% and the internet gap from 25% to 8% — [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/2012291); [DRF](https://digitalrightsfoundation.pk/?p=53474). The figures conflict, possibly because one counts percentage points and the other uses GSMA's relative-gap metric, so check the primary GSMA report. These are adult (18+) figures, not teen figures.
  - **Khalily et al. (2020), "Smartphone addiction and its associated factors among students in twin cities of Pakistan," *JPMA* 70.** 702 school, college and university students aged 12-19 in Islamabad/Rawalpindi; Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version. 60% (422) met the addiction cut-off, and social-networking apps were a significant indicator. This shows high smartphone exposure among urban teens. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5455/jpma.23054)
  - Ejaz, Altay & Naeem (2023), *Digital Health* 9: 427 Pakistani adults. Participants under-reported daily smartphone use by about 11 minutes compared with screen-time screenshots, and "mobile data donation is viable in Pakistan." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/20552076231186075)

### Inferences
- Many girls, and rural students generally, may not own a personal smartphone. They likely use a shared or parent's phone. The app should work on low-end Android, support offline play and short sessions, and offer school-lab or teacher-led modes. A design where the parent's phone is used fits the strong parental role in Q1.
- The urban teen evidence (60% "addicted," social-media heavy) suggests strong game appeal in cities but does not generalise to rural areas.

### Gaps
- No nationally representative data on smartphone ownership or access specifically for ages 13-17, by gender and region, was found (PSLM's 10+ bracket is the closest). Recent PSLM/HIES 2024-25 ICT figures could not be retrieved (the PBS PDF link returned 404).
- No Pakistani study on the acceptance (e.g., TAM/UTAUT) of a gamified career or assessment app among school students was found.

## Q5. Indian/Bangladeshi near-context evidence on gamified or online career assessment for Class 10/12

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed validation study of a gamified career assessment for Indian or Bangladeshi Class 10/12 students. Commercial Indian platforms (e.g., Mindler) market "gamified" assessments, but their validity claims are vendor-only. Near-context academic evidence is limited to ML stream-selection models (Sethi et al.), a DAT aptitude study (Agra), and factor surveys (Bangladesh) that mirror Pakistani findings on parental influence.

### Cited Findings
- **Akter, Jabbar & Khatun, "Factors Affecting Career Choice of the Female Secondary Students in Khulna District of Bangladesh," *Khulna University Studies*** (OpenAlex lists 2022; the DOI suggests 2018). 181 female students in classes 6-10. Most aspired to be doctors, influenced by parents' expectations and self-efficacy. Father's occupation and income and mother's education and occupation were associated with choice; father's education was not. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.53808/kus.2018.15.1and2.1706-s)
- Indian ML stream selection (86.72% accuracy) and the DAT aptitude study: see Q3 — [Sethi et al.](https://doi.org/10.2174/2213275912666181128120527); [Pyari et al.](https://doi.org/10.15415/iie.2016.32011)
- Mindler is marketed as a "gamified career assessment" covering 56 parameters across personality, aptitude, interest, EQ and orientation style, with a 10th-12th track. This is a vendor/blog claim, not peer-reviewed. — [Mindler blog](https://www.mindler.com/blog/the-role-of-psychometric-assessments-in-choosing-the-right-career-in-india/); [Medium listing](https://careercounselling.medium.com/7-best-career-assessment-test-in-india-f876587b0803)
- Systematic reviews useful for intervention design (international, not South Asian):
  - Career interventions for high-school students mainly target career adaptability and decision-making outcomes — [PMC11650720](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11650720/)
  - Middle-school career-readiness evaluations typically use quasi-experimental pre/post designs — [PMC12095145](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12095145/)

### Inferences
- The absence of validated gamified career assessments in South Asia is both a risk (no off-the-shelf validated instrument) and a publishable opportunity. A pre/post quasi-experiment measuring career decision self-efficacy, plus convergent validity against the Career Key Urdu, would be a credible evaluation design.
- Bangladeshi findings mirror Pakistan's (parents, doctor aspiration, parental SES), which supports regional generalisability of the app's design assumptions.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed validation of Mindler, the Univariety/iDreamCareer assessments or the NCERT/CBSE online aptitude tools (e.g., the earlier "Tamanna" initiative) was retrieved. This needs targeted searching.
- No Bangladeshi software or gamified stream-guidance study for SSC/HSC students was found in OpenAlex.
