# Game-based measurement of interests, aptitudes and personality: validation evidence (full-text deep dive)

Scope note: Full texts were read (PDF text extracted) for: Fadillah et al. 2025 meta-analysis; Ramos-Villagrasa et al. 2022 systematic review (HTML full text); Georgiou, Gouras & Nikolaou 2019; Quiroga et al. 2019; Shute, Ventura & Ke 2015 (Portal 2); Rahimi, Shute & Zhang 2021 (Physics Playground persistence, which reports Ventura & Shute 2013 numbers). Items marked [abstract/secondary only] were not verified in full text. Almost all samples are adults or university students. Adolescent samples are flagged explicitly.

## 1. Headline meta-analytic and review evidence (2025 meta-analysis; Ramos-Villagrasa review; Georgiou & Nikolaou)

### Takeaway
The "r ≈ .52" game-vs-questionnaire figure (Fadillah et al. 2025) is actually a pooled Fisher's Z of 0.516. Back-transformed, it is r ≈ .47 (95% CI ≈ .37–.57). It comes from 18 adult/student samples with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 93.9%), and individual studies range from r = .10 to .80. Systematic reviews conclude that game-related assessments give better applicant reactions but no clear psychometric advantage over conventional tests.

### Cited Findings
**Fadillah, Hidayat & Santoso (2025). "Convergent Validity of Game-Based Assessment: A Meta-Analysis." *International Journal of Serious Games*, 12(4), 5–29. DOI 10.17083/etxn7795** — [IJSG article page](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/view/1028); full-text PDF read via [download](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/1028/636/6860)
- Scope: 18 studies from 13 articles, searched in Scopus, ProQuest, Wiley and ScienceDirect. The inclusion criterion was "adults in pre-employment settings", so **no adolescent samples were included**. The self-report criteria were personality measures (Big Five/IPIP/NEO/HEXACO, plus resilience/adaptability scales) — [IJSG PDF](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/1028/636/6860)
- Pooled effect, random effects: **Fisher's Z = 0.516, SE 0.064, 95% CI 0.391–0.642, Z = 8.088, p < .001**. The authors label 0.516 as "r", but the forest plot shows it is the Fisher's Z mean. Back-transformed, it is about r = .47 (CI ≈ .37–.57). This is my calculation (tanh); the paper does not report it — [IJSG PDF](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/1028/636/6860)
- Heterogeneity: **I² = 93.87%, Q(17) = 277.24, p < .001, τ² = 0.0655, τ = 0.256** — [IJSG PDF](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/1028/636/6860)
- Per-study convergent r values with N (Table 2):
  - Myers et al. avatar behavioural-inhibition task: .783 (N = 114) and .780 (N = 210)
  - Georgiou et al.: .448 (N = 97)
  - McCord et al.: .392 (N = 77), .204 (N = 98), .272 (N = 338)
  - Triantoro et al. gamified survey: .360 (N = 694)
  - Georgiadis stealth assessment: .782 (N = 80)
  - Leutner et al.: .420 and .390 (N = 729 each)
  - Barends et al. Honesty–Humility game: .330 (N = 116) and .280 (N = 287)
  - Afroza et al. 3D game: .171 (N = 30)
  - Landers & Collmus storified personality measure: .160 (N = 352)
  - Wu et al. Big Five facet GBA: .101 (N = 142)
  - Hilliard et al. image-based forced choice: .702 (N = 108)
  - Ramos-Villagrasa & Fernández-del-Río: .475 (N = 182)
  - Ramos-Villagrasa et al. 2024 PLoS ONE: .798 (N = 98)
  - Mean ages ≈ 20–40; samples mainly undergraduates and online panels.

  Source: [IJSG PDF](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/1028/636/6860)
- Moderators: none were significant.
  - Sample size: <100 = 0.579; 100–300 = 0.602; >300 = 0.342; Q(2) = 4.40, p = .111.
  - Game type: gamified assessment (k = 10) 0.595 vs. full GBA (k = 8) 0.419; Q = 1.85, p = .174.
  - Number of traits measured: Q = 4.51, p = .341.
  - Data-driven (0.671, k = 3) vs. theory-driven (0.488, k = 15): Q = 0.347, p = .557.

  Source: [IJSG PDF](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/1028/636/6860)
- The authors flag circular validation (validating against the same self-reports a game aims to replace) and recommend IRT and predictive designs tied to behavioural outcomes — [IJSG](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/view/1028)
- Benchmark: an older meta-analysis reports questionnaire-to-questionnaire convergence for the same trait of about .31–.56. That makes .47 comparable to "alternate questionnaire" convergence (cited in the 2025 faking paper's abstract) [secondary] — [J Bus Psychol 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-025-10019-6)

**Ramos-Villagrasa, Fernández-del-Río & Castro (2022). "Game-related assessments for personnel selection: A systematic review." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 13:952002. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002** — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Note: the brief cited this as "2023"; the published review is 2022. It is PRISMA-based, uses WoS + Scopus (searched March 2022), and includes 34 articles (29 empirical). Most are cross-sectional with student samples. Gamified assessments are the largest category (29.4%) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Reliability: the review gives almost no coefficients. It notes that internal consistency is problematic for multi-measure games and recommends test–retest as the alternative (Sanchez et al., 2022) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Convergent validity is "inconclusive":
  - Hilliard et al. 2022: "good" convergence with a personality questionnaire.
  - Harman & Brown 2022: "modest" convergence with the Big Five.
  - Wu et al. 2022: games intended to measure Big Five facets measured cognitive ability better.

  Source: [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Criterion validity:
  - Game-related assessments predicted academic performance in student samples (Nikolaou et al. 2019; Landers et al. 2021; Auer et al. 2022; Hommel et al. 2022).
  - Incremental validity was limited: Owiwi added nothing beyond cognitive ability plus personality.
  - Conclusion: they "do not offer sufficient advantages to recommend their use over conventional methods" unless better applicant reactions are valued for their own sake.

  Source: [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Reactions:
  - Most studies found positive reactions, and framing a test as a "game" improved them (McChesney et al. 2022; Collmus & Landers 2019).
  - Landers et al. 2020/2021 found the gains over conventional tests minimal.
  - A Polish sample preferred traditional methods.

  Source: [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Faking: only one study addressed it (Landers & Collmus 2022, where a storified test was more faking-resistant). Adverse impact was no greater than conventional tests, and sex differences were small — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Mobile/app issues: al-Qallawi & Raghavan (2022) reviewed 9 app-store serious games. Negative reactions came from technical problems (bugs, connection errors) rather than test content — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)

**Georgiou, Gouras & Nikolaou (2019). "Gamification in employee selection: The development of a gamified assessment." *Int. J. Selection & Assessment* 27(2), 91–103. DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12240** (the Owiwi SJT) — [full-text PDF](https://skillup.coreconsulting.it/home/pdfs/Georgiou,%20K.,%20Gouras,%20A.,%20&%20Nikolaou,%20I.%20(2019).%20Gamification%20in%20employee%20selection%20The%20developme.pdf)
- Design: 25 SJT scenarios, each with 4 response options. They measure Resilience, Adaptability, Flexibility and Decision-making, with 6–7 scenarios per construct after expert (20 HR professionals) content matching. The scenarios were converted into adventure "missions" around a common story. The game has a visual progress bar and can be saved and resumed. Construct scores are sums of scenario scores — [PDF](https://skillup.coreconsulting.it/home/pdfs/Georgiou,%20K.,%20Gouras,%20A.,%20&%20Nikolaou,%20I.%20(2019).%20Gamification%20in%20employee%20selection%20The%20developme.pdf)
- Non-gamified SJT (N = 321 Greek business students and graduates), convergence with scales as regression β:
  - Resilience SJT → resilience scale: .350
  - Flexibility SJT → HEXACO flexibility: .366
  - Adaptability SJT → adaptability scale: .166
  - Decision-making SJT → decision-making scale: .389

  Source: [PDF](https://skillup.coreconsulting.it/home/pdfs/Georgiou,%20K.,%20Gouras,%20A.,%20&%20Nikolaou,%20I.%20(2019).%20Gamification%20in%20employee%20selection%20The%20developme.pdf)
- Gamified version (N = 97 subsample, controlling for age and gender), convergence was stronger than for the text SJT:
  - Resilience: β = .565
  - Adaptability: β = .528
  - Flexibility → HEXACO flexibility: β = .552
  - Decision-making: β = .450

  Source: [PDF](https://skillup.coreconsulting.it/home/pdfs/Georgiou,%20K.,%20Gouras,%20A.,%20&%20Nikolaou,%20I.%20(2019).%20Gamification%20in%20employee%20selection%20The%20developme.pdf)
- Structure: CFA on 410 workers and job-seekers gave Satorra–Bentler χ²(269) = 306.94, CFI = .91, NNFI = .89. A cross-validation across N = 731 supported invariance — [PDF](https://skillup.coreconsulting.it/home/pdfs/Georgiou,%20K.,%20Gouras,%20A.,%20&%20Nikolaou,%20I.%20(2019).%20Gamification%20in%20employee%20selection%20The%20developme.pdf)
- Companion paper: Nikolaou, Georgiou & Kotsasarlidou (2019), *Spanish J. Psychology* 22, e6. The game-based assessment predicted GPA beyond personality, and was tested against self-rated job performance and OCB (Greek sample). Coefficients were not extracted — [Cambridge PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/39125B6F3BD27F6B4C1AD514D2330E4A/S1138741619000052a.pdf/exploring-the-relationship-of-a-gamified-assessment-with-performance.pdf)
- Faking study (2025, *J. Business & Psychology*): test-takers could distort game-based personality assessments as well as traditional ones [abstract/secondary only] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-025-10019-6)

### Inferences
- When reporting the meta-analysis, cite "pooled Fisher's Z = .52 (≈ r = .47), I² = 94%". Avoid the bare "r = .52".
- Heterogeneity is so large that any single app needs its own local validation. Expect anything from r ≈ .10 (data-driven Big Five facet games) to ≈ .80 (closely mirrored gamified questionnaires).
- Gamified questionnaires (questionnaire items wrapped in a narrative/visual skin) converge better (.595 vs. .419) than games inferring traits from behaviour. For a 14–18 interest and personality module, the lowest-risk design is the Owiwi pattern: scenario-based items skinned as story "missions", with a progress bar and save/resume.

### Gaps
- No adolescent samples in the meta-analysis. The Fadillah per-trait (Big Five) breakdown was not reported.
- I could not access full text for Landers & Sanchez (2022, *IJSA*, "Game-based, gamified, and gamefully designed assessments for employee selection: definitions, distinctions, design, and validation"). Its taxonomy and validation guidance are known only through citations in Ramos-Villagrasa et al. No numbers were obtained.
- The 2025 faking study's effect sizes were not obtained (paywalled).

## 2. Stealth assessment (Shute et al.): Physics Playground / Newton's Playground, persistence, Bayesian networks

### Takeaway
Shute's stealth assessment of persistence uses log-file time on unsolved or above-average-difficulty levels. It was validated in 8th–9th graders (US), but the convergent correlations with external persistence measures are small (r = .22–.26). This is the most adolescent-relevant evidence, but it shows that behavioural log indicators carry modest validity.

### Cited Findings
- Shute, Ventura & Kim (2013), "Assessment and learning of qualitative physics in Newton's Playground", *J. Educational Research* 106(6), 423–430. **Sample: 167 eighth- and ninth-grade students (US), about 4 h of play over 1.5 weeks** [abstract only] — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/vjerxx/v106y2013i6p423-430.html)
- Ventura & Shute (2013), "The validity of a game-based assessment of persistence", *Computers in Human Behavior* 29(6). In-game persistence was the time spent on unsolved plus solved-but-difficult problems in the 2D physics game. It **correlated r = .22 (p < .05) with an external performance-based persistence task** (as reported in Rahimi et al. 2021 full text) — [Rahimi, Shute & Zhang 2021 PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/HLM.pdf)
- Ventura, Shute & Small (2014): the in-game persistence measure related to posttest scores of low performers (r = .26, p < .05), controlling for gender, game experience, pretest and enjoyment — [Rahimi et al. 2021](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/HLM.pdf)
- Rahimi, Shute & Zhang (2021), *IJTES* 5(2), 141–165, DOI 10.46328/ijtes.118. **N = 137 8th–9th graders, Florida K-12 school.**
  - Persistence was operationalised as time on unsolved problems plus problems solved above average time.
  - In an HLM, problem difficulty (physics-concept and game-mechanics difficulty) predicted persistence time, with concept difficulty γ = .10, t(3,628) = 10.39, p < .001.
  - Gold/silver trophies were the only significant student-level predictors.
  - Persistence time correlated r = −.80 with number of attempts, a construct-overlap caution.

  Source: [PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/HLM.pdf)
- DiCerbo (2014), Poptropica: time on difficult unsolved quests plus tasks completed per quest loaded on one persistence factor in CFA — [Rahimi et al. 2021](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/HLM.pdf)
- Replication (ICCE 2020, Philippines, N = 60 students): the Shute persistence markers (time, restarts, revisits) intercorrelated. Average restarts correlated r = .49 with earning a gold badge. The authors warn the markers may capture non-learning behaviour such as "wheel spinning" — [APSCE ICCE](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/download/1004/939/1836)

### Inferences
- A concrete copyable task design for a 14–18 app's conscientiousness/grit indicator: include a few "very hard / impossible" puzzles and log time-on-task before quitting, along with restarts and revisits.
- Expect only r ≈ .2–.3 with external criteria. Combine with self-report rather than replace it.
- Scale-to-difficulty matters. Persistence time depends heavily on item difficulty, so it must be normed within item (e.g., time relative to item mean) — this is also why Rahimi et al. used above-average-time problems.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain full text for Shute & Ventura (2013) *Stealth Assessment* (MIT Press book) or for Kim, Almond & Shute (2016), "Applying evidence-centered design for the development of game-based assessments in Physics Playground" (*Int. J. Testing* 16(2)). Bayesian-network reliability numbers from these were not verified.
- The Shute lab file "PPReliability.pdf" exists ([pluto.coe.fsu.edu](https://pluto.coe.fsu.edu/svn/common/rgroup-shiny/Papers/PPReliablility.pdf)) but could not be downloaded.

## 3. Cognitive aptitudes via games: fluid reasoning, spatial ability, g (tablet/console)

### Takeaway
Commercial puzzle games played on iPad and Wii U produce game scores with reliabilities of .77–.92. A latent game factor correlated .79 with latent g (adults); an earlier brain-game battery reached .93. Portal 2 play improves mental rotation and navigation. These results support a mobile puzzle battery as an aptitude proxy if many games and normed level-completion scores are used.

### Cited Findings
**Quiroga, Diaz, Román, Privado & Colom (2019). "Intelligence and video games: Beyond 'brain-games'." *Intelligence* 75, 85–94** — [full-text PDF](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)
- Sample: N = 134 Spanish university students (105 women, 29 men), M age = 21.04, SD = 2.23, range 18–30. **Adults** — [PDF](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)
- Games: 10 games, 7 on iPad and 3 on Wii U. The iPad games were Blek, Crazy Pool, Hook, Rail Maze, SkyJump, Space Invaders and Unpossible; the Wii U games were Art of Balance, EDGE and Splatoon. Each was played for 10–20 min under controlled conditions. Untimed training levels came first, followed by timed real levels. The score was the number of levels completed, except points/metres/deaths for three games — [PDF](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)
- Reliability of game scores: **.77 to .92**, compared with .74–.84 for the psychometric tests (fluid reasoning, visuospatial and speed batteries). Hook, Rail Maze, Space Invaders and Unpossible were too hard (floor effects), while SkyJump was too easy — [PDF](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)
- Validity: the latent correlation between g and gVG was **.79** (SEM CFI = .908, RMSEA = .072). Previous work reported latent correlations of .60–.93; Quiroga et al. 2015 reached .93 with a brain-game battery — [PDF](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)
- Literature reviewed in the same paper:
  - A Portal-chamber measure had test reliability .92.
  - Another game measure had reliability .80, with correlations of .61–.63 to intelligence tests and .78 at construct level.
  - Lim & Furnham 2018 found Portal chamber completion time correlated r = .61 with Raven APM.

  Source: [PDF](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)
- Design criteria (Quiroga et al. 2009, as summarised): moderate complexity, low consistency across screens (novelty per level), and no "transfer keys". Correlations are strongest when prior practice is uniform and many games are used [secondary summary] — [ResearchGate 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281317097_Can_we_reliably_measure_the_general_factor_of_intelligence_g_through_commercial_video_games_Yes_we_can)

**Shute, Ventura & Ke (2015). "The power of play: The effects of Portal 2 and Lumosity on cognitive and noncognitive skills." *Computers & Education* 80, 58–67** — [full-text PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)
- Design: 77 undergraduates (US; 218 screened), randomly assigned to 8 h of Portal 2 or Lumosity. Pre/post measures were problem solving (Raven items, insight, remote associates), spatial ability (Mental Rotation Test adapted from Vandenberg & Kuse; Spatial Orientation Test; a virtual 3D navigation task, VSNA) and persistence (latency on impossible picture-comparison trials) — [PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)
- Short-form reliability (copyable for app short forms; split-half, then Spearman–Brown):
  - 12-item Raven: .65 pre / .70 post (≈ .90 at 60 items)
  - 6-item MRT: .65 (≈ .90 at 30 items)
  - 6-item Spatial Orientation Test: .72 (≈ .89)
  - 5 impossible-trial persistence: .64 (≈ .84)

  Source: [PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)
- Results: Portal 2 beat Lumosity on problem solving (F(1,71) = 5.49, p = .02) and on spatial ability (F(1,71) = 4.41, p = .04, d = .64). Persistence (impossible-task time controlling for self-report) also favoured Portal 2 (F(1,71) = 4.00, p = .05, d = .42) — [PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)
- Within the Portal 2 group, MRT improved pre to post (d = .30, one-tailed). VSNA time was faster for Portal 2 (M = 87.31 s vs. 119.94 s; F(1,73) = 6.18, p = .03) — [PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)
- Engagement: enjoyment was 4.32/5 (SD .93) for Portal 2 vs. 3.50 (SD 1.05) for Lumosity, F(1,73) = 12.69, p < .001, d = .83. Enjoyment was controlled in the models — [PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Copyable mobile design for a reasoning/spatial module:
  - 6–10 short (≈10 min) puzzle mini-games (line-drawing physics like Blek, rail/path routing like Rail Maze, block-stacking balance, 3D rotation).
  - Untimed tutorial levels, then timed levels scored as levels completed.
  - Pre-calibrate difficulty, since 4 of 10 commercial games had floor effects in adults and would be worse in younger teens.
- Short 6–12-item spatial and matrix forms have reliability of only about .65. An app needs about 20–30 items per construct (or adaptive testing) to approach .85–.90.
- Practice and game-experience effects (gaming background) are a fairness threat. In Pakistan, gender gaps in gaming exposure may bias spatial-game scores, so record prior gaming and consider controlling for it.

### Gaps
- No adolescent (14–18) game-based matrix or numerical-reasoning validation with correlations against standard tests (e.g., Raven, DAT) was retrieved in this pass. Numerical-reasoning games specifically were not found.
- The Tetris–mental rotation literature was not retrieved in full.
- No phone-specific (small-screen) validation numbers were found. Quiroga used iPad, not phones.

## 4. Game-based measurement of vocational interests (RIASEC)

### Takeaway
Validated game-based RIASEC measures are scarce. A CSU master's thesis (MTMM design) and a 120-person serious-games pilot exist, but neither provided accessible coefficients, and neither is clearly adolescent. The evidence base for game-derived interest scores is thin. Validated short self-report RIASEC instruments for adolescents should anchor any app.

### Cited Findings
- "A validation of a game-based assessment for the measurement of vocational interest" (Cal State scholarworks thesis). The game-based assessment was compared with traditional RIASEC measures in a multitrait-multimethod matrix. Full text was blocked (403/HTML), so no coefficients were obtained [abstract only] — [CSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/concern/theses/v118rg29s)
- "Are serious games a reliable approach to the assessment of vocational interests?" (pilot). N = 120 (90 university students, 30 young adults; 65% women). It aimed to correlate in-game behaviour indicators with interests. The authors conclude the games need better engagement and more discriminative mini-games [snippet only] — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/53379662/Are_serious_games_a_reliable_approach_to_the_assessment_of_vocational_interests)
- A 2025 interactive-narrative serious game embeds RIASEC items as story decisions, integrated with OpenAI. Its rationale is to reduce evaluation apprehension. Psychometrics were not verified [abstract only] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392649003_The_Use_of_Interactive_Narratives_in_Educational_Games_to_Assess_Vocational_Interests_An_Application_of_the_RIASEC_Test_Integrated_With_OpenAI)
- Adolescent-valid non-game anchors:
  - 18REST: an 18-item RIASEC short form validated in Brazilian adolescents and adults, with gender invariance — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6967077/)
  - A Scottish study confirmed the RIASEC circumplex in 1,306 secondary pupils using an online inventory — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8462744/)
  - Iconographic (picture-based) interest inventory 3IP validation — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4873077/)
  - O*NET Interest Profiler reliability/validity report (benchmark for convergence; documents the R–C gap in Holland measures) — [O*NET](https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/IP_RVS.pdf)
  - Gfrörer et al. 2022: interests in early adolescence show both trait and state components — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08902070211035630)

### Inferences
- The safest copyable approach is a "gamified questionnaire" rather than a pure behavioural game: picture- or icon-based RIASEC activity cards (3IP-style), swipe like/dislike, with 18REST-length coverage (3 items per type). This mirrors the meta-analytic finding that gamified questionnaires converge better (.595) than behavioural GBAs (.419, Fadillah 2025).
- Validate the result by checking the circular RIASEC structure (e.g., a randomization test) and convergence with a standard inventory.
- Because adolescent interests have state components (Gfrörer 2022), retest-stability claims should be modest.

### Gaps
- No published convergent-validity coefficients for any game-based RIASEC measure in 14–18-year-olds were found.
- Implicit or choice-based interest measures (e.g., in-game choice frequencies) lack validation data in the sources retrieved.

## 5. Test anxiety, motivation, faking and data quality

### Takeaway
Game framing tends to improve reactions and enjoyment (Portal 2 enjoyment d = .83). One crossover study in tertiary students found game-based exams lowered test anxiety and raised performance. Faking resistance is not established: a 2025 study found game-based personality assessments are fakeable. Reaction gains over conventional tests are sometimes minimal.

### Cited Findings
- Mavridis & Tsiatsos (2017), *J. Computer Assisted Learning*, "Game-based assessment: investigating the impact on test anxiety and exam performance". Tertiary students, 2×2 crossover (3D game exam vs. traditional exam). Test anxiety was lower and exam performance significantly better in the game condition [abstract only; randomisation not confirmed] — [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/97555-game-based-assessment-investigating-the-impact-on-test-anxiety-and-exam-performance)
- A Wordwall review cautions that game-based assessment does not automatically reduce anxiety [snippet] — [UNESA](https://journal.unesa.ac.id/index.php/jpls/article/download/58823/17395)
- The game label improved reactions in McChesney et al. 2022 and Collmus & Landers 2019. Landers et al. 2020/2021 found only minimal gains; a storified measure resisted faking better (Landers & Collmus 2022) — [Frontiers review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full)
- Faking: 2025 *J. Bus. Psychol.* — game-based personality assessment is susceptible to faking similar to traditional tests [abstract] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-025-10019-6)
- Data quality: technical bugs and connection errors drive negative reactions in app-store serious games — [Frontiers review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002/full). Floor and ceiling effects in commercial games (4/10 too hard) — [Quiroga 2019](https://zigzaghr.be/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quiroga_emergent-games_intelligence.pdf)

### Inferences
- In a low-stakes career-guidance app for teens, the motivation and anxiety benefits are plausible but under-evidenced in adolescents. Faking is less of a concern than careless responding and offline/bug-induced data loss. Build offline caching, save/resume (as Owiwi does), and attention checks.

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT on test anxiety with gamified aptitude/interest tests was found. Mavridis effect sizes were not extracted.

## 6. Psychometric engines for games (IRT, Bayesian networks, CAT)

### Takeaway
Evidence-centred design with Bayesian networks is the established stealth-assessment approach (Shute; Kim, Almond & Shute 2016). The 2025 meta-analysis explicitly recommends IRT. However, I could not retrieve numeric reliability or validity results for Bayesian-network or in-game CAT implementations.

### Cited Findings
- Stealth assessment relies on game-log evidence models feeding a competency model (Bayesian networks in Physics Playground), with persistence indicators drawn from time and attempts — [Rahimi et al. 2021](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/HLM.pdf)
- The meta-analysis recommends IRT-based validation and predictive designs over simple Pearson convergence — [Fadillah et al. 2025](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/view/1028)
- Spearman–Brown short-form projections (Shute et al. 2015) show how item counts drive reliability; for example, 6 MRT items give .65, projected to .90 at 30 — [Portal 2 PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/portal1.pdf)

### Inferences
- For an app: use IRT-calibrated item banks for the matrix, spatial and numerical puzzles (level difficulty equals item difficulty) with adaptive level selection. That turns "levels completed" into theta estimates and lets you stop once SE is small enough, shortening sessions on phones.

### Gaps
- Kim, Almond & Shute (2016) Bayesian-network details and numbers were not retrieved.
- No study found reporting CAT embedded in a mobile game for adolescents with validity coefficients.
