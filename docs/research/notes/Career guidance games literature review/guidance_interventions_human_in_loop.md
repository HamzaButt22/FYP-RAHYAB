# Career guidance intervention effectiveness, CACGS, and human-in-the-loop recommendations

Method note: Searches used the OpenAlex API (abstracts reconstructed from the inverted index), the Semantic Scholar API, and web search/fetch. Several classic meta-analyses (Oliver & Spokane 1988; Whiston et al. 1998, 2003, 2017) have **no abstract in OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar**, and their full texts are paywalled. Their findings below come from **secondary summaries** and are flagged. Items marked "abstract-only" were read only at abstract level.

## 1. Meta-analyses of career interventions: effect sizes and the components that matter

### Takeaway
Career choice interventions produce small-to-moderate average effects (roughly d = .30–.60 overall). Effects grow when an intervention combines several "critical ingredients": written exercises, individualized interpretation/feedback, world-of-work information, modeling, and building support. Whiston et al. (2017) add counsellor support, values clarification and psychoeducation. Three or more ingredients beat one or two, and the newest meta-analysis of individual counselling (2025) again found that a small set of components predicts effects.

### Cited Findings
- Brown & Ryan Krane's (2000) five critical ingredients are written exercises, individualized interpretations and feedback, world-of-work information, modeling opportunities, and attention to building support for choices within one's social network. The chapter calls these meta-analytic findings "evidence-based practice" for career-choice help — [Evidence-Based Practice in Career and Workforce Development Interventions (2017 chapter, abstract)](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315714769-3)
- Interventions with **three or more ingredients were "much more effective than those that included only one or two"**. Brown & Ryan Krane did not report significance for each ingredient — [CDAA summary of the evidence](https://cdaa.imiscloud.com/Web/Blog/Posts/What-is-best-practice-in-careers-education--An-outline-of-the-evidence.aspx) (secondary)
- Whiston, Li, Goodrich Mitts & Wright (2017), *J. Vocational Behavior* 100, 175–184, is a meta-analytic replication and extension, cited 248 times in OpenAlex — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2017.03.010). No abstract is available in OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar. The details below come from secondary sources:
  - Participants had about "a 60% chance of attaining a higher outcome measure" than controls, a moderate but significant effect — [CDAA](https://cdaa.imiscloud.com/Web/Blog/Posts/What-is-best-practice-in-careers-education--An-outline-of-the-evidence.aspx) (secondary)
  - The study confirmed written workbooks, personalised feedback and world-of-work information. It added **counsellor support, values clarification and psychoeducation**, which it described as having a greater impact than the original five — [CDAA](https://cdaa.imiscloud.com/Web/Blog/Posts/What-is-best-practice-in-careers-education--An-outline-of-the-evidence.aspx) (secondary). Note: one secondary search summary lists these three as "the" critical elements, so the exact ingredient list should be checked against the original.
  - Repeated interventions beat one-offs. Group formats can match individual ones. **Expert-facilitated interventions beat self-directed or computer-mediated ones** — [CDAA](https://cdaa.imiscloud.com/Web/Blog/Posts/What-is-best-practice-in-careers-education--An-outline-of-the-evidence.aspx) (secondary)
- Overall effect sizes for career interventions tend to fall in the range of about **.30 to .60** (moderate). Individual counselling and career classes had the largest effects. Group counselling, group test interpretation, structured workshops and **computer-assisted interventions** fell in the moderate group — SAGE Encyclopedia of Career Development, "Career intervention outcomes" (seen only as a search snippet; [link](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/careerdevelopment/chpt/career-intervention-outcomes) is paywalled)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of **individual career counselling** (35 samples) found **g = 0.82 for career outcomes** and g = 0.68 for mental-health outcomes. Five components predicted effects: psychoeducation about the decision process, cognitive restructuring, written exercises (occupational analyses), individualized feedback on career choice, and attention to reducing potential obstacles — [J. Counseling & Development 2025 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.1002/joec.12239)
- A school-age meta-analysis (9 studies, N = 1,433) found **weighted mean ES = 0.42 (95% CI 0.19–0.65)**. Gains showed up mainly in career decidedness and in attitudes such as future time perspective — [JEELR 2023 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.20448/jeelr.v10i2.4554). This is a small meta-analysis in a lower-tier journal.
- A systematic review of 25 high-school career interventions found the dominant theories were career construction, SCCT and CIP. The typical format was **group counselling of about 8 sessions × 45 min**, with outcomes mostly in career adaptability and decision-making — [Frontiers in Psychology 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1461503)
- In an RCT with N = 1,034 Finnish 9th graders, a 1-week "Towards Working Life" workshop improved career-choice preparedness, which in turn improved attitudes toward career planning — [Career Development Quarterly 2011 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2161-0045.2011.tb00074.x)
- **Liu, Huang & Wang (2014)**, *Psychological Bulletin*, covers 47 job-search interventions, so it is adult/job-seeker evidence. Participants had **2.67 times higher odds of employment** than controls. Effective components were teaching job-search skills, improving self-presentation, boosting self-efficacy, encouraging proactivity, goal setting and **enlisting social support**. Interventions worked **only when skill development and motivation enhancement were combined**. They were also more effective for younger job seekers — [Psych Bulletin abstract](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035923)
- Oliver & Spokane (1988), "Career-intervention outcome: What contributes to client gain?" (*J. Counseling Psychology* 35(4), 447), is cited 330 times — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0167.35.4.447). No abstract was retrievable. Its commonly cited effect size and its intensity (hours/sessions) finding **could not be verified this session**.

### Inferences
- For an app, the strongest evidence-aligned design is a **bundle** of at least 3 ingredients. Candidates are: (a) a written/reflective exercise; (b) individualized interpretation of results (the "why this fits you" explanation); (c) concrete world-of-work and stream information; (d) modeling, such as short stories or videos of role models, including same-gender role models in counter-stereotypical streams; and (e) a support-building step that brings in a parent, teacher or counsellor. Values clarification and decision-process psychoeducation are cheap to add digitally.
- Liu et al.'s "skills and motivation together" finding suggests pairing information with self-efficacy building, such as mastery experiences in the games, rather than delivering information alone.
- Repeated or multi-session use beats one-off use. The app should be structured as several short sessions rather than a single test, as FindMyself did (see Q2).

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes from Whiston et al. 2017 (overall d, and counsellor-free vs counsellor-involved d) and from Oliver & Spokane 1988 could not be retrieved, because both papers are paywalled and have no abstracts in the open APIs. A university-library pull of the original papers is recommended before the figures are quoted in a thesis.
- Brown et al. (2003, *JVB* 62, 411–428), the follow-up on the critical ingredients, was not retrieved.

## 2. Computer-assisted / online career guidance vs counsellor-delivered; blended models

### Takeaway
Computer-assisted systems work, but on average less well than counsellor-involved interventions. Adding a human (counsellor or workshop) to a CACGS consistently improves outcomes. Newer online interventions, many of them synchronous and counsellor-led, show large effects, especially in collectivist cultures and in brief formats. For adolescents, self-exploration followed by personalised information beats information alone.

### Cited Findings
- Whiston, Brecheisen & Stephens (2003, *JVB* 62(3), 390–410), "Does treatment modality affect career counseling effectiveness?", is summarised by secondary sources as follows. Computer-assisted interventions were **less effective than other modalities**. **Counsellor-free computer use did worse than computer use supplemented by counselling.** Structured group workshops beat unstructured groups — [Frontiers 2024 review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1461503), [CDAA](https://cdaa.imiscloud.com/Web/Blog/Posts/What-is-best-practice-in-careers-education--An-outline-of-the-evidence.aspx) (secondary; exact d values not retrieved)
- A CACGS RCT-style study (N = 609 community-college and university students) compared the CACGS alone, the **CACGS plus a structured workshop**, and business-as-usual. The main effects showed the **CACGS + workshop condition was the effective one** for improving career outcomes. The study also tested moderators such as SES, disability, locus of control and decision readiness — [2007 dissertation abstract (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.25675/3.020683)
- A comparison of DISCOVER, SIGI and SIGI PLUS (N = 132) found all three rated positively. Students who **needed career information** rated them significantly more effective (p < .001) for developing and evaluating options — [FSU technical report 1987 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.17125/fsu.1525882239); journal version: [Computers in Human Behavior 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0747-5632(94)90002-7)
- A **2026 meta-analysis of online career counselling** (14 studies, N = 1,721, 2016–2025) found a **large effect, g = 1.65 (95% CI 1.17–2.13)**. Outcomes covered adaptability, career decision self-efficacy (CDSE), decision status and others. Moderators: **stronger effects in collectivist contexts and in briefer interventions (≤6 sessions)**. Heterogeneity was substantial, and pre-experimental designs were included, so the pooled g is probably inflated — [J. Career Assessment 2026 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.1177/10690727261433253)
- In a Chinese 10th-grade randomized study (N = 957; 601 intervention vs 356 control), a synchronous online CIP-based programme combined career courses, interest and academic self-concept assessments, and a university-majors database. It significantly increased career readiness (maturity, vocational identity) and reduced decision difficulties — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.815076)
- A 2026 RCT in lower-secondary school found that **self-exploration of interests followed by a personalised ordering of career information** increased information acquisition. Students also **read about more diverse career paths** and shifted attention toward routes that do not require university — [RCT, 2026 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.1086/742919)
- A labour-market-information RCT (n = 203, job centres) randomized independent self-help versus counsellor-assisted use. The LMI effect over time was significant in both groups but **larger when assisted by a counsellor** (moderate clinical effect) — [Canadian Journal of Career Development 2021 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.82396/cjcd.v17i1.3123)
- Users of Gati's internet-based MBCD system reported higher perceived benefit when they were **satisfied with the length and variety of their personal "promising alternatives" list**. Decidedness was associated with decidedness at the end of the dialogue — [JVB 2003 (abstract fragment)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0001-8791(02)00049-0)
- The **FindMyself** mobile app was developed through a user-centered design process (field study N = 46). Its authors warn that online guidance systems "bear the risk of imposed decisions" because they neglect non-cognitive processes. The app used multiple short sessions with challenges and reflection tasks, plus discussion with peers, and was rated easy, flexible and fun. **Some users asked for specific career recommendations** — [ACM MUM 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3626705.3627774)
- In a US high-school study, use of a Career Information System (CIS) was examined against vocational skills self-efficacy, outcome expectations, work hope, planning and decision difficulties — [2019 study (abstract-only; findings not in snippet)](https://openalex.org/search?q=Career%20Information%20System%20Utilization%20High%20School)

### Inferences
- Evidence favours a **blended model**: the app provides assessment, information and reflection, and a human (school counsellor, teacher or trained parent) reviews and discusses the result. A structured "discuss with your counsellor/parent" step, ideally with a printable or shareable summary, is the digital analogue of the "CACGS + workshop" condition.
- Run self-exploration (games/inventory) **before** showing stream information, and order the information by personal fit, per the 2026 RCT. This also broadens the options students consider.
- Online interventions appear to work well in collectivist settings, which is encouraging for Pakistan, but the effect sizes are likely inflated by weak designs.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found of a fully self-directed mobile app for **stream/track choice** with outcome follow-up, such as persistence or satisfaction a year later.
- No Pakistani or South Asian CACGS outcome study was found.
- Long-run outcomes (academic persistence, choice satisfaction after 1+ years) are rarely measured in this literature; most outcomes are proximal (CDSE, decidedness).

## 3. Responses to algorithmic/AI career recommendations; parent involvement, especially in collectivist and South Asian contexts

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on adolescents' trust in algorithmic career advice is thin and partly unpublished. Students may accept algorithms for "objective", grade-based advice but prefer humans for interest- and values-based advice. Users also preferred a gender-stereotyped recommender over a debiased one. Parent-targeted interventions can shift adolescents' course-taking and later STEM pursuit. Parental career-related behaviours (support, interference, lack of engagement) are measurable and predict exploration and indecision.

### Cited Findings
- In a study with 200+ college students, a **gender-debiased career recommender** was fairer offline without loss of accuracy. Yet users on average **preferred the original biased system**, and **perceived gender disparity determined acceptance**. Fair algorithms alone are therefore insufficient — [arXiv 2021, User Acceptance of Gender Stereotypes in Automated Career Recommendations](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2106.07112)
- Monachou (Yale) ran a registered experiment with 1,100 Greek high-school students. Identical college recommendations were attributed either to a counsellor or to an algorithm. Outcomes were adoption, trust (intent and competence), understanding of the rationale, and goal compatibility. **The registry lists no results** — [AEA RCT Registry AEARCTR-0014368](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/14368). A secondary summary of a related talk says students showed aversion to algorithms when the basis was objective but not when it was most subjective. That claim came only from a search snippet of a mailing-list post and is **unverified and appears to conflict with another snippet**, so it should be treated as preliminary.
- A **parent-based utility-value intervention**, consisting of brochures and a website helping parents explain why maths and science matter, increased high-school STEM course-taking. In follow-up it raised ACT maths/science scores by **12 percentile points**, and it was linked to greater STEM career pursuit **5 years later** — [PNAS 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1607386114)
- A German parent-based utility-value intervention was likewise aimed at helping parents support adolescents' career orientation — [Journal of Educational Research Online/ZfE 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42010-018-0024-x) (no abstract retrieved; findings unknown)
- The **Parental Career-related Behaviors (PCB) questionnaire** (support, interference, lack of engagement) was validated in Italian with N = 481 adolescents. **Parental support was most strongly related to adolescents' career exploration**, and interference and lack of engagement were linked to career indecision — [TPM 2016](https://doi.org/10.4473/tpm23.3.6). It was replicated in adolescents (N = 323) and university students (N = 374) — [Current Psychology 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-022-02764-1)
- In a sample of N = 838 senior secondary students in underdeveloped China, a person-centred analysis linked profiles of parental support, intervention and engagement to career adaptability — [IAFOR 2024 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.22492/issn.2186-5892.2024.113)
- In Indonesia (a collectivist setting; N = 317 vocational students), **perceived student–parent career congruence** and psychological capital predicted employability development — [Heliyon 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e13214)
- A Pakistani survey (Khairpur, Sindh; N = 320) examined how parental education, family size, income and guidance shape students' educational career choices — [JPSA 2026 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.63468/jpsa.4.2.37). The earlier report already covered the figures that 82.6% of students rely on parents and about 30% are not allowed to choose.
- In Lebanese mixed-methods research, parents reported stress and uncertainty about AI, labour-market volatility and a generational information gap. They expressed a need for support in guiding children — [AASoci 2025 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.4236/aasoci.2025.157033)
- A Canadian "Partners Program" study of parent–adolescent dyads (critical incidents, 302 incidents from 9 dyads) identified facilitating events in a structured parent-involved career programme — [UBC thesis 2010 (abstract-only)](https://doi.org/10.14288/1.0053724)

### Inferences
- For Pakistan, parents are effectively co-decision-makers. The app should treat the parent as a **second user**: a parent report framed around supporting exploration (PCB "support") rather than interference, with talking points modelled on utility-value messaging such as "why this stream connects to careers you value."
- Because users may prefer stereotype-consistent recommendations, debiasing the algorithm is not enough. The **presentation** must actively counter stereotypes, for example through counter-stereotypical role models and explicit statements that the stream fits regardless of gender.
- Pending real data, assume adolescents and parents may over-trust a "scientific" app score. Frame outputs as inputs to a conversation, not verdicts.

### Gaps
- **Keller & Whiston (2008)** (parental career-related behaviours and young adolescents' career development, *J. Career Assessment*) was not retrieved this session, so its specific findings are not cited.
- No peer-reviewed study was found on **adolescents' or parents' trust in, over-reliance on, or explainability needs for AI career recommendations in South Asia**.
- No RCT of a parent-involvement career intervention in Pakistan or India was found.

## 4. Presenting results: top-N vs single recommendation, uncertainty, identity foreclosure, gender stereotypes

### Takeaway
There is no direct experimental evidence comparing top-N with a single recommendation for adolescent career or stream choice. Indirect evidence favours a short, varied shortlist (users value list length and variety), personalised ordering that broadens exploration, and avoiding "imposed decisions". Gender-stereotyped outputs are both harmful and readily accepted by users. Teacher track recommendations carrying stereotypes measurably steer girls into less demanding tracks.

### Cited Findings
- Satisfaction with the **length and variety of the "promising alternatives" list** was associated with higher perceived benefit in an internet career decision system — [Gati et al., JVB 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0001-8791(02)00049-0)
- In Comparing & Choosing (a free internet decision-support system), 412 users' intuitive top choice was compared with the outcome of a systematic comparison of the occupations on their shortlist. This supports a design of **shortlist first, then structured comparison** — [J. Career Assessment 2024 (abstract-only; findings truncated)](https://doi.org/10.1177/10690727241287532)
- Personalised ordering of information after self-exploration led adolescents to read about **more diverse** paths — [2026 RCT](https://doi.org/10.1086/742919)
- Online guidance systems "bear the risk of imposed decisions." A reflection-first app was well received, though some users wanted explicit recommendations — [FindMyself, ACM 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3626705.3627774)
- Latent profile research identified **five types of career indecision**. Some people still consider many alternatives while others have narrowed to a few or one, so typing users can guide which intervention to offer — [J. Counseling Psychology 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/cou0000603)
- Teachers' implicit gender-science stereotypes (measured by IAT) widened the maths gap. They led girls to **self-select into less demanding high schools, following teachers' track recommendations** — [Carlana, QJE 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz008)
- Users preferred a biased career recommender over a debiased one — [arXiv 2021](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2106.07112). A separate study offers a metric for measuring gender stereotype reinforcement in ranking algorithms — [Information Processing & Management 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2020.102377) (no abstract retrieved)
- SAIL-Y (Colombia) is a gender- and socioeconomic-aware recommender built to raise female STEM participation using national test data. It is a design example, not outcome evidence — [Electronics 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics14204121)
- Identity foreclosure is defined as "teenagers commit to their roles without exploring" — [EAI 2024](https://doi.org/10.4108/eai.18-7-2023.2343401). Most empirical foreclosure–career work found involves student-athletes, where foreclosure is linked to lower career maturity — [JHETP 2022](https://doi.org/10.33423/jhetp.v22i7.5286)

### Inferences
- Show a **ranked shortlist of about 3 streams** with reasons and explicit uncertainty, not a single verdict. This matches the "promising alternatives" evidence and the earlier report's design. Add a structured comparison step (C&C-style) and an explicit "explore more" path to reduce foreclosure risk.
- Use **non-deterministic language** ("strong fit based on what you showed today", "results can change as you grow") and allow re-takes. This counters both over-reliance and premature commitment.
- Audit outputs by gender: compare recommendation rates by gender for students with matched scores. Pair recommendations with counter-stereotypical role models, since stereotype-consistent advice from authority figures (teachers) shifts track choice.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found that compares single vs top-N career recommendations, or uncertainty-display formats, for adolescents. The recommendation above is inferential, drawing on decision-support and recommender HCI literature that was not retrieved here.
- No adolescent study was found showing that receiving an algorithmic career recommendation causes identity foreclosure. The risk is theoretical, based on Marcia's identity status model.
- The choice-overload literature (e.g., Iyengar & Lepper) was not checked for career-specific replications.

## 5. Ethical guidelines (NCDA, IAEVG, ITC) for technology-based career assessment for minors

### Takeaway
All three bodies have relevant guidance, but the texts were only partly retrieved. NCDA issued a new Code of Ethics in 2024 that incorporates long-standing technology guidelines. IAEVG revised its ethical guidelines in 2025 with explicit attention to AI, fairness, transparency and autonomy. ITC has computer-based/internet testing guidelines (2005/2006) and joint ITC–ATP technology-based assessment guidelines (2022). Minor-specific clauses were not verified.

### Cited Findings
- The NCDA released a new Code of Ethics edition in June 2024. Its earlier technology guidelines had been folded into the 2007 Code, and the technology section was reviewed against other associations' codes — [NCDA news](https://www.ncda.org/aws/NCDA/pt/sd/news_article/565370/_PARENT/layout_details/false); [NCDA code PDF](https://www.ncda.org/aws/NCDA/asset_manager/get_file/3395) (full text not parsed)
- Kettunen & McMahon (2025), "Revision of IAEVG ethical guidelines," appeared in *IAEVG Magazine* 97, 5–6 (not peer reviewed). According to a search summary, it addresses AI in guidance with attention to **fairness, transparency and client autonomy**, organised into five core areas — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/105041) (abstract not on page; content summary is secondary)
- The ITC Guidelines on Computer-Based and Internet-Delivered Testing (2005/2006) and the ITC/ATP Guidelines for Technology-Based Assessment (2022) exist — [Cambridge chapter on ethical/legal concerns in internet testing](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/next-generation-technologyenhanced-assessment/ethical-and-legal-concerns-in-internetbased-testing/08A3780CE735F4FE567035C440F4905F) (secondary)
- The earlier report already cites the UK Age Appropriate Design Code requirement that profiling of under-18s come with age-appropriate explanations and prompts to talk to an adult.

### Inferences
- The common ground across these guidelines, which the app should operationalise, is: validated instruments only, with norms appropriate to the population; transparency about how results are generated; the client's autonomy to accept or reject results; human professional oversight of interpretation; data protection; and fairness checks. For minors, add parental consent and adult involvement.

### Gaps
- Exact clause text (e.g., NCDA Section on technology, and any provisions on minors or AI) was not retrieved. The NCDA 2024 PDF and the ITC/ATP 2022 guidelines should be read directly before they are quoted.
- IAEVG's five core areas were not verified from a primary source.
